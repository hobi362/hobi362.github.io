An 1800s writing desk that changes on cue. At the right moment in a live-action adventure,
its top rotates and swaps one set of props for another — revealing the clue that tells the
team how to escape the room.

### The brief

Our client was [5 Wits](https://www.5-wits.com/), a company that builds live-action
adventure venues where a group walks into a themed room and has to solve their way out.
We were designing an effect for their "Mystery Manor" adventure, set in a mansion in the
late 1800s. The effect had to act as a clue: at the right moment in the story, a writing
desk would change, revealing the prop that tells the team how to escape.

The hard constraints came from the venue, not from the physics. It had to survive being
triggered dozens of times a day by paying customers, it had to look like period furniture
and not like a machine, and it had to read clearly as *magic* from across a dim room —
which meant the transition needed to be fast and the mechanism needed to be invisible.

![Annotated concept sketch: a motor hidden in the desk drawers rotates the desktop, bringing a concealed box of instructions up into view](/assets/images/courses/product-design/concept-sketch.jpg)

### Sketch modeling

Most of the semester was spent building rough physical models rather than drawings. The
point of a sketch model is to answer one question fast and then get thrown away, and this
class was where I got comfortable doing that instead of over-refining a CAD model before
testing anything.

![Four-frame sequence of a cardboard-and-wood sketch model, a rotating bookshelf tested as an early version of the reveal](/assets/images/courses/product-design/sketch-model-sequence.png)

We tried the reveal several ways — a shelf that rotates, a surface that slides, a panel
that flips — before settling on rotating the desktop itself about its long axis, so that
the underside of the desk becomes the new top.

### The mechanism

A stepper motor mounted inside the desk pedestal drives the tabletop shaft through a
flexible coupler. Hiding the entire drive inside the existing drawer volume was the
constraint that shaped the whole design: nothing could protrude, and the desk had to
still read as furniture with the effect at rest.

### What I took from it

This was the first design course I took where "is it correct?" was the wrong question and
"does it land for the person in the room?" was the right one. The user-centered process
and the habit of sketch modeling — build the cheap version, learn one thing, move on —
carried directly into how I approach medical device work, where the person holding the
device matters at least as much as the mechanism inside it.
